SQLUp: data questions, answered in Slack

HackerRank · 2025

Three agents, one Markdown handbook, and a threaded reply in under 5 minutes.

<5 minfrom a 3 to 4 day turnaround

The queue

Data questions at HackerRank came into a public Slack channel, #data-support. They queued up, and the usual turnaround was 3 to 4 days.

Most of them were simple to answer once someone sat down to write the query. The wait was the problem.

Three agents, one handbook

SQLUp was a Metabase app installed in Slack. Someone dropped a question into #data-support in plain English, and it went to the SQL agent.

The SQL agent worked from a set of Markdown files: business rules, how to read each dataset, and the filters every query has to apply. Basically a handbook for writing SQL the way we’d write it.

It built the query and ran it on our data through Metabase. The result came back as a CSV.

A second agent, the analytics agent, read the CSV and worked out the answer to the actual question.

A third, the validator, checked that answer. It never saw how the analytics agent got there, only the original request and the business rules. It acted as an LLM judge: does the math hold up, and is anything made up?

The answer landed as a Metabase document, with a TL;DR posted as a threaded reply to the original question.

How a question became an answer

What changed

3 to 4 days became under 5 minutes, and answers hit 75% accuracy. Every answer came with its full working in the Metabase document, so anyone could check it.

Grading a text-to-SQL agent

I also designed the evaluation framework for HackerRank’s internal text-to-SQL analytics agent. Every answer got scored on three things: business alignment, functional correctness and interpretability.

We tracked Pass@1 and Pass@N on top. Pass@1 is the one-shot success rate: did the agent get it right on the first try? That sounds strict, since we usually give LLMs some room to hallucinate. With this agent we held a higher bar, because its context window was comparatively small. We wanted Pass@1 to be high.

Pass@N was the fail-safe. If the first try missed, did more querying still get people to the answer they were after, or did it go completely off track? A healthy Pass@N meant we were heading in the right direction. Pass@1 was the best possible outcome.

The findings went to the AI team, and they drove the fixes for how the agent handled ambiguous business questions.

Grading a text-to-SQL agent

  1. Business alignmentDid it answer the question the business was actually asking?
  2. Functional correctnessIs the SQL right, and are the numbers right?
  3. InterpretabilityCan a person follow how it got there?
